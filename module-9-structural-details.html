The skin isn't the structure

Modules 1–2 were about one laminate: fiber + resin, anisotropic, oriented on purpose for a known load path. A real hull is that laminate wrapped as a shell around an internal skeleton — stringers, floors, bulkheads, a transom, and the joints holding it all together. This module is that skeleton.

Field notes: core transitions

The curriculum also flags "core transitions" — the spot where a cored panel meets solid laminate, which happens right around joints like #4 and at any hardware cutout. The core has to stop and the skins have to be laminated solid together there, sealed so water can never reach the cut edge of the core. Module 10 covers this in detail for thru-hull fittings specifically; the short version here is the same principle that runs through this whole module — get the transition/joint right, or it becomes the failure point.

The formal version of this same judgment call is a scantling calculation (ISO 12215 is the usual reference) — it turns "trace the load path, pick the fiber direction" into a number. You don't need the math to make the call correctly; a shop's engineer or a scantling table does the arithmetic once you already understand why it's asking these questions.

Field notes: hat-stiffened construction, and designing for a failure you can actually see

Hat-stiffened construction is often used on commercial and military boats built for rough seas, for a specific reason: when it fails, it tends to fail first at the stiffener's attachment point to the hull. That sounds like a downside, but it's actually the design intent — a failure right at the visible attachment joint is easy to notice and easy to repair, versus a hidden structural failure buried somewhere else in the hull that nobody sees until it's serious.

Where two moldings become one boat

Pick a joint type, read the notes

The hull-to-deck joint is one of the most heavily loaded seams on the boat — it has to carry torsional (twisting) forces, which increase as the boat's beam gets wider. Select a joint geometry to see how it's typically built.

Torsion and beam: whichever geometry is used, the joint has to be built to handle the twisting loads for that boat's beam — a joint that was fine on a narrower hull can be under-built on a wider one.
