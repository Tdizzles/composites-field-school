The one idea everything hangs on

What a composite actually is

A composite is two materials that, together, beat either one alone: a reinforcement (the fibers) locked inside a matrix (the cured resin). Tap each below and watch what you'd actually get.

This division of labor is the thing 80% of the exam quietly tests. Hold onto it.

Field notes: where this actually started

What launched modern composites: phenolic resin. Fiberglass boatbuilding began after WWII, when the US Navy contracted a 28-foot personnel craft built from it, chasing reduced maintenance versus wood. It proliferated around 1960 as lower hull costs and near-zero maintenance drove a boom in ownership.

Early FRP builders had no structural calculator — they used the empirical method (build it, test if it survives), which is exactly why early fiberglass boats are often over-built. The three advantages that made composites win: higher specific strength (strength per pound), the ability to be molded into complex, seamless shapes, and greater durability than planked wood.

Anisotropy

Strong only where the fibers point

Steel is equally strong in every direction. A laminate is not — it's anisotropic. Drag the load and watch its strength collapse as it points across the fibers instead of along them.

Field notes: how you actually pick fiber direction on a real part

Trace the load path. If you can point to one line on the part and say "this only ever gets pulled that way" — a stringer cap, a keel, a mast compression post — that's a single, known load path, and unidirectional fiber laid straight along it is the efficient answer.

If the part is loaded from several directions or you can't be sure (a hull skin taking hoop, longitudinal, and slamming loads) — multiple/uncertain load paths — you cover every direction with a quasi-isotropic layup (0°, 90°, and both ±45°), so no direction is left for the resin to carry alone.

The math behind the slider above: each ply's usable strength in a given direction falls off roughly with cos²θ of the angle between the fiber and the load (classical laminate theory resolves this with a transform matrix per ply). A ply 45° off the load keeps roughly half its strength; at 90° it contributes almost none — which is exactly the curve the slider is tracing. This single relationship is the whole reason ply orientation is chosen on purpose rather than by habit.

Fiber-to-resin ratio

More glass, less resin — up to a point

Resin is the heavy, brittle part. More glass and just enough resin to fully wet it = strong and light. Too much resin is dead weight; too little and you can't wet the fibers, so voids form. Find the sweet spot.

Strength-to-weight86
Good wet-out: strong and light.

Real ratios depend on process (hand lay-up runs leaner than vacuum infusion). Trend shown, not a target number.

Calculating it for real: percent fiber content = (weight of fiber ÷ total weight of the cured laminate) × 100%. That single calculation is how a shop actually checks whether a laminate hit its target ratio, rather than eyeballing it.
