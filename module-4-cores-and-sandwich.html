Cores & sandwich construction — start here

The core has exactly one job

As Module 1 showed, a lightweight core holds two thin skins apart and carries shear between them — that separation is what multiplies bending stiffness, the I-beam principle, for almost no added weight. The core is not there to be strong on its own. Watch what happens when that bond fails.

Field notes: why does debonding matter so much in the real world?

A skin-to-core bond can fail from day one — poor wet-out or consolidation during layup, not enough resin or pressure to actually bond the skin to the core — or it can develop over time, from water intrusion degrading the bond or a hard local impact separating the two.

Either way, once the skin and core separate, the core stops doing its shear job. That section of panel behaves close to two thin, floppy skins with dead weight stuck between them — exactly what the meter above shows when you flip to "debonded."

Field notes: kerfs — how rigid core sheets follow a curved hull

Kerfs (scoring cuts) let a rigid core sheet bend to follow curved hull and deck shapes without breaking. But every kerf is also a cut edge that exposes raw core right under the laminate — if those kerf channels aren't fully filled with laminating or bedding resin, they become a wick path for water straight into an otherwise sealed core. Full encapsulation of every cut edge, kerfs included, is what keeps a balsa or foam core dry for the life of the boat.

Field notes: why sandwich failures are harder to catch than solid-laminate damage

This is part of why solid laminate is flagged as easier to inspect for structural damage: a crack in a solid laminate is usually visible right on the surface. A sandwich failure often hides between two intact-looking skins — a debond, a sheared core, or a crushed core can all sit under a surface that still looks fine, which is exactly why systematic checks (not just a glance) matter on cored hulls and decks.

The "why" is simple: water only needs one unsealed path to a core edge to start wicking sideways through it, and that damage is invisible from outside until it's advanced. That's the same reasoning behind sealing every kerf cut (Section 2) — an exposed core edge anywhere is a way in for water.
