Field notes: is CSM even used much, and why not just use biax everywhere?

Chopped strand mat — yes, constantly, but never for strength. It's a bulk/bond layer: builds thickness cheaply, blocks print-through of a coarser weave underneath gelcoat, and — because its random short fibers and extra resin fill the gaps — bonds adjacent plies of woven roving together. The classic hand-lay marine schedule is literally roving/mat/roving/mat for this reason.

Why not biaxial everywhere? Cost and process. Stitched fabric costs more per pound than roving or mat, and it can't be sprayed — chopper-gun/spray-up (a large share of production boatbuilding) physically lays down chopped roving, not a stitched sheet. For bulk, non-critical, or sprayed work, the cheaper forms still win on cost and labor time even though they're weaker per pound.

Field notes: why bother with multiple glass types at all?

Each fiber is a different point on the cost/strength/stiffness/toughness/weight trade-off curve — there's no single "best" glass, only the best one for a given zone of the boat. E-glass covers the vast majority of structure because it's cheap and easy to wet out; the exotics earn their keep only where their one specific property (S-glass's stiffness, carbon's stiffness-to-weight, aramid's impact toughness) is worth the added cost and handling difficulty.

Galvanic corrosion with carbon: carbon fiber conducts electricity and sits far from common boat metals — especially aluminum — on the galvanic series. Direct contact between a carbon layup and aluminum hardware, in the presence of moisture/seawater as an electrolyte, can drive rapid corrosion of the metal (carbon effectively behaves as a noble cathode). Fix: isolate carbon layups from metal fittings with a glass barrier ply, epoxy isolation, or non-conductive fasteners/bushings — never bed carbon directly against aluminum.

Field notes: where woven roving is actually used, and why mat goes between layers

Where it's used: woven roving is the default heavy reinforcement for large marine structures — hull bottoms and big panels — because it builds thickness fast (24 oz/yd² is the most common weight) and drapes well enough over hull curves. Woven roving is a flattened bundle weave with slightly more material in the warp (lengthwise) direction than the fill.

"Pre-buckled" / crimp: because the bundles physically weave over-and-under the perpendicular bundles, each fiber is already bent before any load is ever applied. Under tension, that bent fiber has to straighten out first before it can carry full force — so you get more stretch and lower ultimate strength per weight than a straight (stitched) fiber of the same spec.

Why mat between layers: roving's coarse weave leaves poor contact between plies on its own. A layer of chopped strand mat between roving plies fills those gaps with random short fiber and extra resin, giving a real interlaminar bond and also helping block print-through. That's why the classic schedule alternates roving/mat/roving/mat rather than stacking roving directly on roving.

Field notes: single vs. multiple load paths — how to actually decide

Ask: can you point to one line on the part and say "this only ever gets pulled that way"? A stringer cap, a keel, a straight compression post — yes, single/known load path, so a unidirectional or dominant 0° layup is efficient. A hull skin, a transom, a chine taking slamming loads from unpredictable angles — no, multiple/uncertain load paths, so biaxial/triaxial or a full quasi-isotropic layup is the standard, safer choice. This exact judgment call is what a structural scantling calculation (e.g. ISO 12215) formalizes with numbers; as a builder you can make the same call by eye once you know what to look for.

Heavier fabric: more glass, harder to wet

Reinforcement is sold by weight per area — oz/yd² in the US, gsm elsewhere. Heavier means more glass and faster build, but it drapes worse and is harder to wet out fully. Slide it.

Decode "1708": a 17 oz/yd² ±45° biaxial stitched to a 0.75 oz chopped mat backing ("08"). The number literally tells you the weight and the construction. (1 oz/yd² ≈ 34 gsm.)
Field notes: when is cloth used most, and what is a "weave" / "styrene binder" exactly?

Cloth is a fine, tight weave — typically 6–10 oz/yd², needing roughly 40–50 plies to build one inch of thickness. Because it's light and gives a smooth, low-texture surface, it's used for small parts, repairs, and cosmetic/finish layers, not for building structural bulk.

A weave is the physical pattern created when perpendicular bundles — the warp (lengthwise) and the weft/fill (crosswise) — are interlaced over and under each other. That interlacing is what creates crimp (Section 3).

The styrene binder: chopped strand mat is held together by a binder applied to tack the randomly-cut fibers into a sheet. That binder is formulated to dissolve in the styrene monomer present in polyester and vinylester resin, letting the mat collapse and wet out properly. Epoxy has no styrene, so with epoxy the binder stays stiff and the mat won't wet out — the CSM+epoxy trap from Section 1.
