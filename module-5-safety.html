Safety, health & the shop environment — start here

Everything else in this course is optional if you get hurt

Every other module is about making a good part. This one is about walking out of the shop the same way you walked in. It's also one of the most heavily tested sections on the ABYC exam — the questions are specific, and "close enough" answers are wrong answers.

The chemicals you're actually dealing with

Read the diamond before you read the label

Most resin, gelcoat, and solvent containers carry an NFPA HazCom diamond. Four quadrants, four colors, always the same meaning — learn this once and you can read any container in the shop.

Blue = Health Red = Flammability Yellow = Instability (reactivity) White = Specific hazard
Shop environment basics, fast: the SDS (Safety Data Sheet — formerly MSDS) is the reference document for any chemical's hazards, handling, and first aid; keep them accessible. A large spill means immediate containment, alert management, and keep it out of floor drains. Solvent-soaked rags go in a sealed, fire-rated container — they can spontaneously combust. Moving heavy objects with a hoist requires two employees, no exceptions.
Field notes: why does this module get so much exam weight?

Because the failure modes here aren't "the part looks bad" — they're "someone goes blind" or "the shop catches fire." MEKP-in-the-eyes and MEKP-plus-accelerator are the two classic ABYC trap questions: they sound like chemistry trivia until you realize the wrong answer is a hospital trip. Treat this section the way the exam does — as the non-negotiable one.

Know your enemy

Four hazards, four responses

Tap a material to see what it actually does to you, what stops it, and which quadrant of the diamond lights up.

Sanding dust isn't a HazCom-labeled chemical — it's a physical hazard. It still needs the right PPE, just not the diamond.

Gloves, quickly: nitrile resists solvents and resin far better than cotton or leather, which offer essentially no chemical barrier at all. For anything involving MEKP, resin, or acetone, nitrile (or better) is the default — not cotton, not leather.

The distinction that gets tested every time

Match the task to the protection

An organic-vapor (OV) cartridge respirator and a particulate respirator are not interchangeable. One stops vapor, the other stops dust — wearing the wrong one is the same as wearing nothing.

All matched. If you can place these five without thinking, the OV-vs-particulate question on the exam is free points.
Field notes: what actually breaks a respirator's protection

Three things determine whether a respirator is actually protecting you: how it's stored when not in use (a cartridge left open in a dirty shop degrades and gets contaminated), the cartridge type — matched to the real hazard, OV for vapor, particulate for dust, and facial hair, which breaks the seal against your skin no matter how good the respirator is. Any one of those three failing means the respirator isn't doing its job even though you're wearing it.

The one rule that can put you in the hospital

MEKP and cobalt accelerator never touch each other directly

MEKP is an organic peroxide. Cobalt accelerator is an oxidizer. Combine them neat — straight together, not through the resin — and they can react violently. The fix is procedural, not exotic: catalyst and accelerator each go into the resin separately.

Ventilation is the primary control for the styrene vapor both resin and MEKP work releases — good airflow doesn't replace this rule, but it's your baseline protection while you follow it.

If it happens, you won't have time to look this up

MEKP in the eyes: put the response in order

MEKP can cause blindness on eye contact. There's no clever technique here — just speed and follow-through. Put these steps in the right order.

This page confirms immediate copious flushing followed by prompt medical attention. It does not confirm a specific flush duration.
