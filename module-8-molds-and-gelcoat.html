Molds, tooling & gelcoat — start here

Gelcoat goes in first — before the boat exists

Everyone thinks of gelcoat as "the color." It isn't. It's the first material sprayed, into an empty mold, before a single strand of glass goes in. Whatever that mold surface looks like, every part pulled from it looks like too.

Every imperfection on the plug reproduces onto the mold face — and every imperfection on the mold face reproduces onto every single part pulled from it. There is no fixing a bad mold part-by-part; you fix the mold.
Mold, defined: a frame or model around or on which something (a part) is formed or shaped. Production boat molds are typically made from fiberglass composite materials themselves.
No matter what a plug is made from — wood, foam, machined tooling board — its surface must have a smooth gelcoat finish, because every surface flaw on the plug carries all the way through to the finished part.
Field notes: draft angle, framing, and mold inspection

Draft. A mold releases cleanly (independent of any wax or chemical) only if it has a positive draft margin of at least 2° — a slight taper so the part can pull free without being locked in by an undercut — or the mold is built as a split mold that opens in sections around a shape that can't draft in one piece.

Framing. The structural framework built around a mold exists to make sure the mold holds its original shape through repeated part construction and cure cycles — a mold that flexes or sags produces parts that are off-spec even if the gelcoat and layup are perfect.

Before every use, a visual mold inspection checks for heat damage, nicks, scratches, cracks, and surface contamination — because, again, whatever is wrong with the mold is wrong with the part. And never rinse a gelcoated mold with soapy water — residue interferes with release and surface finish.

Recall focus: gelcoat is sprayed in-mold, first — before any structural fiber goes in. It's a manufacturing step, applied under controlled conditions, not a paint coat added at the end.
Field notes: what a "skin coat" actually does

The skin coat is the first layer of fiber laid directly against the cured gelcoat — applied by chopper gun or hand lay-up. It acts as a corrosion barrier that increases resistance to blisters, and it supports/backs up the gelcoat film so the thin gelcoat layer isn't left unsupported against the mold.

Before the bulk structural laminate goes on top, the skin coat should be checked for adequate Barcol hardness development — confirming it has cured enough to safely build more layers on top of it.

Objective: explain mold release (wax vs. PVA)

Nothing gets laid up until release is covered

Release agents let the finished part separate cleanly from the mold. There are three common types — tap each to see what it is and when it's used.

The one rule that never changes: whichever product you use, there must always be sufficient release agent covering the whole mold surface before a part is laid up — application specifics depend on that product's own instructions.

Field notes: why release matters this much

Release agent problems don't show up as a cosmetic flaw — they show up as a part that won't come out, or worse, one that comes out too early (pre-release, covered in Section 5). Both are production-stopping, expensive failures, which is why release gets checked before every single layup, not just when a mold is new.

Field notes: what gelcoat is actually doing for you, and how it's mixed/sprayed

Function: gelcoat is a protective layer, a cosmetic finish, a water-tight barrier, the pigment/color layer, and it aids release from the mold — five jobs from one sprayed film.

Resin type: gelcoat is typically an isophthalic polyester resin, formulated with no surfacing agent (unlike laminating resin, which needs one to cure tack-free in open air — gelcoat cures against the closed mold face instead).

Mixing & thinning: gelcoat should be mixed every 24 hours, for about 10 minutes, to keep pigments and fillers in suspension. If it needs thinning, that's done with acetone — sometimes called a "false monomer" because it thins the viscosity without taking part in the cure the way styrene does.

Flow rate for spraying gelcoat is set by three things together: spray-tip size, gelcoat viscosity, and pump pressure. Before spraying, inspect fittings, lines, and filters for leaks.

Field notes: safety, cleanup, and equipment upkeep around gelcoat

PPE for gelcoat: safety glasses, a properly cartridge-rated respirator, and gloves. For trimming and grinding afterward: a properly rated (particulate) respirator plus eye and skin protection.

Spills & labeling: a red label on a drum means flammable. A large spill must be contained immediately, management alerted, and the material kept from reaching floor drains — the EPA is the regulatory body governing this. Controlled spraying (vs. open/uncontrolled) reduces emissions, reduces overspray, improves transfer efficiency, and cuts waste.

Equipment: an internal-mix spray gun must be flushed after every use. Robotic gelcoat application's main advantage is consistency of thickness and coverage part-to-part. After cure, demold with hooks, straps, and wedges rather than prying on the laminate — and only once the part has cooled to room temperature. A hoist, when used, is a two-employee job. Interior gelcoat is applied only after cutting, grinding, and sanding are complete, and the most common trim method is simply using the mold flange as a guide.
