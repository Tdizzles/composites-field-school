Quality assurance — start here

Good laminate, or a problem waiting to surface?

Everything in Modules 1–10 was about building the laminate right. This module is about proving it was built right — and diagnosing it when it wasn't. Same three questions every time: is the fiber:resin ratio in range, has it cured enough to keep working, and does it look/sound/read clean?

All three panels are the same fiber and resin. The only difference is how well the laminate was consolidated — even resin distribution, trapped air rolled out, layers fully compacted and wetted — while it was being built.

Field notes: why QA is a separate skill from lay-up

A laminator's job is to build the part. A QA check's job is to prove the part matches spec — with a number or a test, not a feeling. That's the whole shift in this module: instead of "that looks about right," you calculate percent fiber content, you check Barcol hardness before adding layers, and you inspect with a defined method (visual, tap-sounding, moisture meter) instead of eyeballing it. Every defect covered here — porosity, alligatoring, pre-release, crazing, dry spots, delamination — has a known, specific cause. Diagnosing correctly means you fix the process, not just the part.

Field notes: the defects in plain language

Porosity — small trapped air bubbles/pinholes in the gelcoat or laminate. Caused by high-speed or turbulent mixing, spraying with too much pressure, spraying too close to the surface (blowing air in), or water contamination from the air or mold surface.

Alligatoring — a cracked, reptile-skin surface pattern in gelcoat. Caused by gelcoat applied too thin, or structural laminate applied too early over it before the gelcoat reached adequate cure.

Pre-release — the part separates from the mold too early. Caused by too much catalyst, too much promoter/accelerator, excessive film thickness, an elevated shop or mold temperature, or an improper release agent.

Crazing / cracking — fine stress cracks in gelcoat. Caused by gelcoat that's too thick or over-catalyzed, which cures too fast and shrinks more than it can handle without cracking.

Dry spots / resin-starved — patches of fabric that never got fully wetted out. Caused by not applying enough resin, or not properly consolidating the laminate (rolling out air and working resin through the fabric) during lay-up.

Delamination — layers separating from each other instead of acting as one laminate. Caused by a poor interlaminar bond: laminating over a fully-cured surface without sanding and a solvent wipe (missing the "green window"), trapped air/voids blocking full ply-to-ply contact, or a lamination schedule that was changed from the design intent.

The QA math

The fiber:resin ratio, as a pass/fail check

Module 1 showed the wet-out sweet spot. Here it's a QA number: shops calculate percent fiber content to confirm a laminate actually hit its target ratio, and watch for the same thing visually — voids forming when there isn't enough resin to fully wet the glass.

The formula: percent fiber content = (weight of fiber ÷ total weight of the cured laminate) × 100%. This is the actual lab/shop check — not a visual guess.

Checking cure before you commit to more layers

Barcol hardness: how hard has it actually cured?

A Barcol hardness tester presses a spring-loaded indentor into the laminate and reads how deep it penetrates. Less penetration (a harder surface) means more cure has developed. Slide through the cure timeline and watch what the indentor does.

Indentation depth (illustrative — less depth = harder / more cured)deep

Why check it at all: the skin coat should be checked for Barcol hardness development before more layers are applied on top — building bulk laminate on top of an undercured skin coat can trap uncured resin and risks a weaker, unpredictable bond.

Field notes: why hardness, not just "does it feel tacky"

Tack-free isn't the same as cured. Curing runs through phases — gel/pot-time (minutes), post-cure (a few weeks), full-cure (up to about a year for full properties) — and a laminate can feel dry to the touch well before it has developed real hardness. A Barcol reading gives an actual, repeatable number instead of a finger test, which is why it's the standard cure-verification check before committing more material on top.

Field notes: what each method actually is

Visual inspection — the first and simplest check: look for cracks, blisters, discoloration, surface contamination, and other visible surface defects. It's the same logic used for mold inspection (heat damage, nicks, scratches, cracks, contamination) applied to the finished part.

Tap / sounding test — tap the surface (e.g., with a coin or a small hammer) and listen. A sharp, solid sound means the laminate is fully bonded underneath; a dull or hollow sound at a given spot suggests a possible void or delamination beneath the surface at that spot.

Moisture meter — reads moisture content non-destructively. An elevated reading suggests water intrusion — for example into a cored laminate, or as an early precursor to osmotic blistering — before it's visible from the outside.
