Curing chemistry — start here

You don't just "add hardener." You control a reaction.

Module 3 showed you that more MEKP ≠ more strength. This module is the deeper mechanics behind that rule: what's actually happening chemically, how mass and temperature change the outcome, and exactly when a fresh layer can still chemically weld itself to the last one.

Polymerization (esterification): individual resin molecules cross-link into one continuous network — liquid becomes solid. It's a one-way, exothermic (heat-releasing) reaction, same as Module 3's thermoset definition, just now happening in real time in front of you.

The redox pair: MEKP (methyl ethyl ketone peroxide) is the catalyst — it initiates the free-radical chain reaction that cross-links polyester/vinylester resin. A cobalt promoter (accelerator), already mixed into most promoted laminating resins, works alongside MEKP to get that free-radical reaction going at working temperatures — that's the "redox pair." Module 5's warning stands: never mix MEKP and cobalt together neat, outside the resin — concentrated contact between the two is a fire/explosion hazard.

Recap from Module 3 — air inhibition: atmospheric oxygen stops surface styrene from fully curing, so a structural (non-final) polyester/vinylester layer stays tacky after its gel time passes. That's expected — it's exactly what lets the next wet layer bond to it. Only the final coat needs a wax/surfacing additive to seal out air and cure hard. Keep that in mind: it's the same window of chemical opportunity behind this module's green bonding window in Section 5.
Field notes: inhibitor vs. catalyst vs. promoter — who does what

Inhibitor — already in the resin from the manufacturer. Slows polymerization so the resin has shelf life and you get working/open time before cure takes over. You don't add this; it's baked in.

Catalyst (MEKP) — what you add at the bench. It initiates the free-radical reaction. It doesn't get "used up" to build strength; it just starts and paces the reaction.

Promoter (cobalt) — usually pre-mixed into the resin by the manufacturer. It's what lets MEKP initiate cure at normal shop temperatures instead of needing heat. Three different jobs, three different chemicals — mixing up which does what is a common exam trap.

Still true from Module 3: the usual everyday working range is 1%–2.5% MEKP by weight; the full allowed band is wider — about 1%–4% — and where you sit in that band is a temperature call, not a strength call. More MEKP shortens working time and raises exotherm; it doesn't make the part stronger.
"Triggering": if a batch suddenly starts gelling far faster than expected while you're still applying it, that's called triggering — and it's a sign of an improper resin-to-catalyst ratio, not bad luck. Weigh or measure your catalyst; don't eyeball it.

Heat does the rest of the work

Thin film vs. thick mass: the exotherm trade-off

Cure is exothermic — it releases its own heat, and that heat speeds up the reaction further. A thick mass traps that heat; a thin film loses it to the air. Slide from a thin film to a thick pour and watch what happens.

Relative exotherm index (thickness-driven)low
Cure-speed multiplier vs. the thin coat1.0×

The exotherm index scales with thickness because generation scales with volume while heat escapes mainly through the top surface — real heat-transfer behavior, illustrative units. The speed multiplier applies the Arrhenius "Q10" rule of thumb (reaction rate roughly doubles for every fixed jump in temperature) to that index — a real, general chemistry relationship, not a resin-specific number.

Demold rule of thumb: whatever the mass, don't pull a part from the mold until it has cooled back to room temperature — pulling it while still hot from exotherm is a common cause of warping.
Field notes: why "just add a few more plies at once" backfires

Laying up too many plies in one session stacks resin mass fast, and that mass traps exothermic heat faster than it can escape. The results show up in different ways depending on the part: cracking or crazing in the laminate itself, pre-release from the mold surface, mold damage from the heat, or a warped/distorted part as it cools unevenly. None of that is a catalyst problem — it's a mass-and-heat problem, which is why the fix is usually "build it in stages," not "use less MEKP."
