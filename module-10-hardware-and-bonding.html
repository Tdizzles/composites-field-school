Hardware installation & bonding — start here

The hole is the easy part. Sealing it isn't.

Every winch, cleat, stanchion, and thru-hull means putting a hole in the laminate. Do it right and the boat is stronger where you drilled. Do it wrong and you've built a permanent leak, a crack waiting to happen, or a rot pocket in the core. ABYC exam area: Hardware installation.

Same hole, two very different problems. The rest of this module is: choose the right fastener, seal the core, bed the fitting, and — if carbon's involved — keep it away from bare metal.

Four skills, four sections ahead: fastener method & backing plates · isolating core from fasteners · bedding/sealing at any penetration · carbon-fiber galvanic risk.

Interactive: pick the fastener

Self-tapping screw, or through-bolt with a backing plate?

The general rule: a self-tapping screw only grips a thin layer of laminate — fine for a light, non-structural, low-load fitting. Anything carrying real structural or shock load needs a through-bolt with a backing plate (also called a bracing plate), which spreads the bolt's load over a much wider area of laminate and resists pull-out.

Interactive: put the steps in order

Isolate the core before a fastener ever touches it

On a cored (sandwich) hull, the core must never directly contact a fastener or a thru-hull fitting. Exposed core next to a hole is a wick — water travels straight into it. The fix is a specific sequence, not a shortcut. Drag the steps (or use the arrows) into the correct order, then check.

The cured epoxy plug is what the fastener actually threads into — not the core. The surrounding core stays sealed off from water on both sides of the laminate.

Skip this and: improper thru-hull installation lets water intrude into the laminate — and on a cored hull, that water wicks through the exposed core, spreading rot far from the original hole. (On single-skin hulls, the equivalent failure is skipping the doubler that restores the strength cut away by the hole.)

Interactive: toggle the bedding compound

Why bedding compound matters at any hull penetration

Every fitting sits on top of a hole. Without something sealing that interface, water tracks under the fitting's base and down along the fastener shanks and threads — a slow, hidden leak path called crevice intrusion. Toggle bedding compound on and off and watch the path close.

Bedding compound isn't just glue for the fitting — it's the seal that keeps crevice water out and lets the fitting and hull flex slightly against each other without opening a gap.

Bedding compound vs. putty — don't mix them up: putties are for filleting bulkheads and stringers (the radius-fillet tabbing joint, as Module 9 covered) — a structural bonding job. Bedding compound seals a hardware penetration against water. Different jobs, different products.

Interactive: tap each hardware situation

Carbon fiber and bare metal hardware don't mix

As Module 2 covered: carbon fiber is electrically conductive and sits far from common boat metals — especially aluminum — on the galvanic series. Bolt a carbon layup directly to an aluminum fitting, add moisture or seawater as the electrolyte, and you get rapid corrosion of the metal — the carbon behaves as a noble cathode. It's a hardware-installation problem as much as a materials one: the fix has to happen at the fastener. Tap each situation below.

Rule of thumb: never bed a carbon layup directly against aluminum hardware. Isolate with a glass barrier ply, epoxy isolation, or non-conductive fasteners/bushings — pick one, don't assume it's fine because the joint looks dry today.
