Manufacturing processes — start here

Same laminate, wildly different part

Everything in Modules 1–6 — the fiber, the fabric, the resin — still has to actually get turned into a boat. How you do that changes cost, consistency, glass:resin control, emissions, and how much skill it demands. Start with the biggest split: open vs closed molding.

For the exam: the two kinds of open molding are hand lay-up and spray-up (chopper gun). Open molding — not any closed method — is still the most commonly used lamination process in the boatbuilding industry.
Field notes: what happens before any glass even touches the mold

Regardless of which process you pick, production follows the same general sequence: mold preparation → release agent → gelcoat → skin coat → structural laminate → cure → demold. The process you choose (hand lay-up, spray-up, vacuum bagging, infusion, RTM, prepreg) changes how the "skin coat" and "structural laminate" steps get done — everything before and after stays the same.

Field notes: how resin actually gets onto (or into) the fiber

Resin application tools: a brush, a resin-fed roller, a spray gun, or a flow coater. Spray guns themselves come in several types — siphon cup, cup gun, pressure pot, high-pressure airless, air-assist airless, HVLP, impingement applicator, and flow coater. "Impingement" means two or more streams (resin and catalyst) are shot so they collide and mix on the way to the surface. A flow coater works like a showerhead: several resin streams merge into one fan, and the orientation of the nozzle holes sets that fan's shape.

Consolidating a laminate — by hand, roller, or vacuum — means evening out the resin, working out trapped air, and rolling the fibers down so the layers are fully compacted and wetted. Every process on this page is, underneath the different equipment, still solving that same problem.

The environmental piece

What actually drives emissions

Open-mold styrene emissions come from resin and gelcoat sitting exposed to open air. The exam-tested detail: it's the exposed surface area that sets the emissions level — not how thick the laminate is. Try both sliders and watch the meter.

Illustrative meter — built to show the ranking rule (area moves it, thickness doesn't), not a certified emissions figure.

Why closed molding wins here: enclosing the resin/catalyst reaction inside a sealed mold or vacuum bag dramatically cuts styrene VOC/HAP emissions versus open molding. Closed molding's other advantages: improved surface finish, reduced material waste, and better control over the fiber-to-resin ratio.
Field notes: infusion vs. RTM — both "closed," not the same thing

Infusion (VARTM — vacuum-assisted resin infusion) is the closed-molding procedure defined by vacuum pressure only — no injection pressure. Dry reinforcement goes into the mold, gets sealed under a bag, and resin is pulled through it by vacuum alone.

VARTM as a broader term (Vacuum-Assisted Resin Transfer Molding) covers processes using vacuum and/or pressure — so RTM, which injects resin into a closed rigid mold, can add injection pressure on top of vacuum. An RTM mold is a two-part tool that has to seal, which is why RTM mold maintenance specifically includes checking that gaskets are clean and intact, on top of basic mold care.

Set it up right before you pull the trigger

Chopper-gun calibration, in order

Spray-up is fast — and it's the most operator-dependent and resin-rich process on this page. A big part of controlling that starts before you spray a single pass: calibrating the gun in the right order. Drag or use the arrows to put these three steps in sequence.

When it goes wrong — three chopper-gun defects, and the fix:
  • Excessive wet chop → increase air pressure to the chop motor.
  • Spring-back (chop doesn't cut cleanly) → caused by dull chopper blades.
  • Improper chop pattern → caused by incorrect gun orientation relative to the surface.

"Triggering" during resin application — the mix starting to kick early or not kicking at all — points to an improper resin-to-catalyst ratio, not the chopper gun itself.

Prove the ratio, don't eyeball it

Calculate percent fiber content

The formula the exam wants: percent fiber content = (weight of fiber ÷ total weight of laminate) × 100%. Slide the fiber and resin weights and watch it compute.

Why this number matters: closed-molding processes (vacuum bagging, infusion, RTM, prepreg) give better control over this exact ratio than open molding does — one of the main reasons production shops move toward them. Two other ways shops protect the ratio: pre-cutting reinforcement fabric (speeds up lamination time, ensures consistency between parts, and allows structural optimization) and overlapping fabric at seams, which transfers stress between adjacent pieces instead of leaving a gap in the load path.
Field notes: the skin coat — why it gets checked before you build on top of it

The skin coat — applied by chopper gun or hand lay-up — is a critical layer: it acts as a corrosion barrier (blister resistance) and supports the gelcoat film. Before any bulk structural laminate goes on top of it, the skin coat should be checked for Barcol hardness development, to confirm it's cured enough to build on.
