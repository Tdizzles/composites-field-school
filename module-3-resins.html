Field notes: what's actually in each resin

Polyester = an unsaturated acid (maleic anhydride) + a saturated acid (isophthalic or orthophthalic) + a glycol (ethylene glycol) + a monomer (styrene). Isophthalic polyester has better mechanical properties, better chemical resistance, and more resistance to water permeation than orthophthalic — at a higher cost. Gelcoat is typically an isophthalic resin with no surfacing agent.

Vinylester = an unsaturated acid (methacrylic or acrylic) + a bisphenol diepoxide backbone + an unsaturated monomer (styrene) — it borrows epoxy's backbone but cures like polyester. That's why it lands between the two: much better blister resistance and secondary bonding than polyester, at a lower cost than full epoxy.

One resin family worth knowing for the exam even though it's rarely a whole hull: phenolic resin is inherently fire-retardant because its chemistry incorporates water — and it's also the resin that launched modern composites in the first place.

Secondary bonding

Not every resin sticks to every resin

Laminating one resin over another already-cured resin is a one-way street. Pick a base (already cured) and what you want to laminate over it, and see if it bonds.

Laminating over it with:

The one-way rule, in one line: epoxy bonds well to nearly everything (including cured polyester/vinylester); polyester and vinylester bond poorly to fully-cured epoxy unless it's heavily sanded and prepped. Epoxy is the resin of choice for secondary/structural bonding and repairs for exactly this reason.

The ratio myth-buster

MEKP catalyzes — it isn't a hardener you add "more of" for strength

Polyester/vinylester cure by free-radical reaction: MEKP just starts (initiates) it. More MEKP does not make a stronger part — it only cures faster and hotter, and can ruin the laminate. (Epoxy is different: it's a stoichiometric two-part reaction, and getting that ratio wrong under-cures it — see the callout below.)

Shop rule of thumb used here: 1 oz ≈ 30 ml. Typical working range is 1%–2.5% MEKP by weight, adjusted down in hot shops and up in cold ones — 4% is shown only to demonstrate the danger zone.

Myth-buster: doubling the catalyst does not double the cure or the strength — it mostly shortens working time and raises exotherm, risking cracking, warping, or pre-release. The usual ratio is 1% to 2.5% (and can range 1–4% depending on temperature); an inhibitor in the resin is what gives you working time by slowing polymerization in the first place.
